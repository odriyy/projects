Trash? CAN!

SC02 Group 1 · DTI Project ESP32-CAM Automated Sorting IoT / Fill-Level Monitoring Product Design

An automated waste-sorting bin designed for the SUTD hostel, built to lighten the load on cleaning staff. An ESP32-CAM identifies what's been thrown in, a stepper motor rotates the correct compartment underneath, and ultrasonic fill sensors report bin status straight to a cleaner-facing app — so rounds happen only when a bin is actually full.

Trash Day, Without the Guesswork

Our problem statement: how might we create an effective cleaning solution to improve hygiene standards and efficiency for cleaning staff? Walking the SUTD hostel, we mapped footfall, odor hotspots, and bin usage across Blocks 55, 57, and 59, and found residents were outnumbering general bins roughly 10 to 1 — with over half of all waste being food scraps left unsorted in a single general bin. Trash?CAN! answers this with an automated sorter that separates waste at the point of disposal and reports its own status, so cleaning staff stop making rounds to bins that aren't full and stop manually sorting bins that are.

How It Sorts

01

Sensing Mechanism

Trash is dropped into a single intake. An ESP32-CAM mounted beside the compartment reads the material and sends the classification to the main ESP32 microcontroller.

02

Trash-Based Rotation

A second ESP32 drives a stepper motor that rotates the hexagonal bin cluster so the correct compartment lines up directly beneath the drop chute.

03

Disposal & Fill Reporting

A servo-controlled bottom lid drops the trash into the aligned compartment. An ultrasonic sensor tracks the rising level and updates the Trash?CAN! app the moment a bin turns "full."

From Cardboard Rig to Finished Prototype

Concept sketch → cardboard-and-breadboard rig → first CAD → finalized prototype with extended display.
  • Initial Concept Sketch: Laid out the basic hexagonal, modular form envisioned for the sorting bin.
  • First Physical Iteration: A cardboard-and-breadboard rig built to prove the spinning parts could rotate reliably before committing to CAD.
  • First CAD Model: Hexagonal bins designed for automatic sorting once all trash was inserted, with interactive elements added to boost engagement.
  • Developed Prototype: Switched to sorting items one at a time for control, swapped the IR sensor for an ESP32-CAM, and added a transparent top cover to let light reach the camera.
  • Finalized CAD: Extended the screen display for easier interaction and switched to recycled material in place of virgin PLA.

Stepper Motor Rotation

The first rig struggled to rotate its spinning parts reliably. Rebuilding around a dedicated ESP32-controlled stepper motor gave the compartment cluster precise, repeatable alignment under the drop chute.

Sensor Misclassification

The IR sensor and proximity sensor in the first prototype often read the bin's own material instead of the trash, giving 0% accuracy on tissue and battery. Replacing it with an ESP32-CAM and a transparent top cover for natural light lifted every category to 50%+.

Toppling Compartments

Modular hexagonal compartments kept toppling when detached for emptying. Detachable hexagonal doors were added instead, letting cleaning staff empty a compartment without lifting or destabilizing the whole cluster.

One Item at a Time

Sorting after all trash was inserted at once made classification unreliable. Switching to a one-item-at-a-time flow gave the camera a clean, single-object view and was the single biggest driver of the accuracy jump between prototypes.

A Day With Trash?CAN!

  • 7:00am — Cleaning Staff: Checks the Trash?CAN! dashboard for fill levels before starting rounds; the pantry bin isn't full yet, so it's skipped for now.
  • 9:00am — Student: Rushing to breakfast with a half-finished cup, unsure where it goes — the bin's face and cheerful sound guide the disposal.
  • 10:00am — Cleaning Staff: Gets a push notification — "Block 59, Level 5, Dry waste full" — and clears just that bin, with no hourly walk-through needed.
  • 5:00pm — Cleaning Staff: One last dashboard check before clocking off — the day ran smoother, with far fewer wasted rounds.
